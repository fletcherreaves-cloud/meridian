What "Difference" means in these tables

Every number below has two layers, not one. The first is this year against the same calendar days last year — matching day for day cancels out season, day of week, and anything else that would move a restaurant's numbers regardless of McValue. The second is after launch against before launch — it isolates what changed specifically around 21 April, rather than a level a restaurant was already sitting at either way. "Difference" is that second layer: how much the year-over-year gap moved, not the year-over-year gap itself. (The formal name is a difference-in-differences, or DiD — worth knowing if anyone in the room asks how this was built.)

Oklahoma and Florida below are each measured this way separately — Florida's number is never subtracted from Oklahoma's. It's shown alongside as a check: if the shortfall were about how Oklahoma's restaurants are run, Florida — a different market, a different District Officer — wouldn't need to move at all. It moved further. The one place two groups are genuinely subtracted from each other is the thirteen-day price test further below, where that subtraction is the whole point.

Florida says the cause is not Oklahoma

Six Florida restaurants, a separate District Officer, nine hundred miles away, a different competitive set — and they fell further. Whatever is happening is not local execution and not one operator. State the caveat before the FBP does: these markets share an ownership umbrella and a person above both. It is a strong control, not a clean one. Saying so first costs nothing.

The price change, measured

Product Mix holds every item's price, per restaurant, per day. A menu price change is a price that moves and stays moved — as distinct from a promotion, which moves for a day and comes back. Requiring a price to hold flat for fourteen trading days on both sides of a change separates the two cleanly.

Run that across January to August and three price rounds fall out, and nothing else does:

The thirteen days that do the work

Between 13 and 26 June, half the district was on the new prices and half was not. Both halves had McValue 2.0 running the whole time. The only systematic difference between them is price.

Read the left-hand column before the right. The two groups sit at an identical −2.83 % before either one moves, then separate by nearly three points the moment one takes price and the other doesn't. That is about as close as observational data gets to a controlled test, and it was sitting in the data the whole time.

The mechanism matters more than the number

Mid-June carries a seasonal lift. The restaurants that hadn't repriced caught it and went positive against last year. The restaurants that had repriced didn't move at all.

Price didn't push traffic down. It stopped traffic going up. That is the version of this loss nobody notices on a report, because nothing ever looks like it got worse — it just quietly stops getting better while everyone else's does.

What we did to try to break it

Two checks, both run, both reported whatever they returned:

  1. Remove the two restaurants that could be driving it Tishomingo, which improves on its own for reasons explained below, and Elgin, the one restaurant genuinely growing. Both sat in the not-yet-repriced group. Removing them made the effect larger — −2.74 pp instead of −2.23 pp. They were damping it, not creating it.
  2. Re-run the whole test on a period when neither group had moved If the two groups drift apart on their own, the result is worthless. They drift by +0.31 pp — about a ninth of the effect, and in the direction that would hide it, not manufacture it.

Scaled across the post-launch window — the first wave carried new prices for 60 of its 112 days, the second for 47 — that is 1.2 to 1.5 points of the 3.96. Report it as a range. The design does not resolve it more finely than that, and pretending otherwise is how a number gets taken apart in the room.

−3.14 pp is the number to put in front of the FBP

Six weeks. McValue and nothing else. No promotion to argue about, no price change to be accused of, no correction applied and no assumption made. Every other figure in this document needs a sentence of defence; this one does not.

It is also slightly worse for us than the corrected full-window estimate of −2.5 to −2.8 pp. Lead with it anyway. A number that needs no defending beats a better number that does.

What the gap after 2 June actually is

Traffic deteriorates by 1.31 points between the clean weeks and everything after. Our June price increase was in force for roughly three-quarters of that stretch, and on its own accounts for 1.8 to 2.3 points of it — more than the entire deterioration.

One tempting conclusion is wrong, and worth naming directly

Blocks 4 through 8 carry six national promotions and both June price rounds — the period with the most promotional support running exactly where traffic looks worst. It would be easy to read that as "traffic got worse as marketing support increased." That reading does not survive the price test above.

Say it in the room and the reply is one sentence long: you raised prices in June. That reply is correct — the June price increase alone accounts for more than the entire deterioration between the six clean weeks and everything after. The decline after 2 June is our price, not the offer failing harder. Which is exactly why the six clean weeks are the number that matters.

The pre-window, tested for a hidden promo

A national, month-long free-item offer — 10-piece McNuggets, $1 minimum — ran in March 2026, inside the pre-launch window used to compute every Difference figure above. An offer like that would inflate pre-launch traffic and depress pre-launch check purely by giving people a reason to come in cheap, which would make the post-launch decline look larger than it really is. That is the one mechanism that could still undercut −3.14 pp, and it was cheap to check directly rather than argue about.

Compare March 2026 against the rest of the pre-launch window (January, February, 1–21 April), each using its own last-year twin:

A live confound predicts a traffic spike and a check dip in March. Both came back the opposite way — traffic 1.10 pp lower than the rest of the window, check 9¢ higher. That is not what a promotion inflating the baseline looks like; it is closer to the offer not moving behaviour detectably in either year, in either direction.

The pre-launch window is not shown to be inflated

The −3.14 pp headline needs no further correction on this account.

Not a finding: average check

An initial estimate carried +10.4¢ as a McValue effect. Measured properly against the pre-launch trend it is +5.1¢, half the figure — and the price dates now explain the timing directly. Check sat at its pre-launch level through the first six weeks and stepped up in the block containing 13 June. That is the block the first price wave lands in.

This is not our number to claim. Handing the FBP a check gain that turns out to be our own price increase costs more credibility than the point is worth. Drop it, and say why we dropped it — a document that retires its own best-sounding number is a document whose remaining numbers get believed.

One restaurant grew

The district figure hides a 17.7-point spread. Per restaurant, the traffic difference runs from +6.34 pp to −11.34 pp. Exactly one restaurant is genuinely positive: Elgin, at +6.34 pp — growing before launch, growing faster after.

That is worth putting in front of the FBP, and worth being honest about: one restaurant out of twenty-six is not a pattern, and Elgin is a travel-stop location whose customer base is not the same as the others'. It is a lead to pull, not a defence.

Tishomingo looks positive and is not — this correction matters

Tishomingo ranks second best in the district at +4.06 pp. Read the two halves: −10.58 % before launch, −6.51 % after. Both deeply negative. It is not improving; it is lapping a less inflated base.

The restaurant opened 16 December 2024, so "last year" for the pre-launch window is months two through five of its life — peak grand-opening — and "last year" for the post-launch window is months five through nine, already decayed. The improvement is the honeymoon fading out of the comparison.

This sharpens why it is excluded. Not "the restaurant is in its opening ramp" — it is twenty months old. Its last-year comparison is the opening ramp.

Supporting: it is not an execution problem

  • Satisfaction held throughout. The slope turned positive after launch and every dimension held during the FIFA promotion, accuracy peaking in June. If restaurants were failing customers, this is where it would show.
  • The FIFA Happy Meal did not move traffic — owner-confirmed as a commercial miss, and corroborated by satisfaction holding right through it. Demand, not execution.
  • Florida fell further under a different District Officer.

Four independent lines now, counting the price test, and none depends on another. Challenging one does not collapse the argument. They converge on the same place: this is demand-side. That moves the conversation from your operators need to execute better to the offer is not working.

Limitations, stated first

  • The post-launch window is not a clean treatment period. Six national events and two price rounds sit inside it. That is precisely why the six clean weeks are the number to lead with.
  • The price test is thirteen days across two groups of unequal size, and the groups were not randomly assigned. Directional and bounded, which is why it is reported as a range rather than a point.
  • Florida took the June price rounds too, in both waves. So price does not explain why Florida is worse — something else does, and we have not identified it.
  • The 2026 calendar was not symmetric with 2025's — March carried a free-item promotion with no confirmed 2025 counterpart. Tested directly rather than left as a bound (see "The pre-window, tested for a hidden promo," above): traffic and check both moved the opposite way from what that confound would predict, so it does not explain the finding. The full 2025 calendar was never pulled — this is a direct measurement of the outcome, not a comparison of the underlying calendars — so treat this as evidence the confound is inert, not proof no calendar difference exists anywhere in the window.
  • Earlier traffic estimates are retired, not reconciled. They were computed over different data and do not reproduce. Nothing from that set appears above.

Two exclusions, both documented

  • Tishomingo — last-year comparison is an opening ramp. Excluding it strengthens the finding.
  • Ponce de Leon — opened 13 March 2026, no last-year comparison exists at all.

The ask

Three ways to frame what's being requested — shaped by the data, but the choice is a business and relationship decision, not something the analysis can make on its own.

  1. Relief, not blame. Lead with −3.14 pp and the four converging lines of evidence, and ask the FBP what levers exist on their side — marketing weight, offer design, timing — rather than asking them to fix something in the restaurants.
  2. A specific request tied to the number. If there is a known relief mechanism (a co-op marketing adjustment, a different offer structure, a review of the national calendar's promotional density), name it and attach the −3.14 pp figure to the ask directly.
  3. A joint diagnostic, not a one-way ask. Propose reviewing the offer together against the district's numbers, positioning this document as the opening data point in an ongoing conversation rather than a closing argument.

The line built to be retold

For six weeks after launch there was no promotion running and no price change in our restaurants. Traffic fell 3.14 points against last year. That is McValue on its own, with nothing else to blame and nothing subtracted — and everything that happened after June is our own price, which we measured before you had to ask.
