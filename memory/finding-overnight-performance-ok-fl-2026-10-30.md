Oct 30, 2026 · @Fletcher Reaves

Closing the night-shift performance gap is worth real money in Florida and a real service fix in Oklahoma. Florida is running about $21,000 a month in excess night labor cost district-wide — roughly $254,000 a year if left unaddressed — concentrated in three stores. Oklahoma's night gap isn't a labor-cost problem; it's slower drive-thru speed at night, and the fix is putting an experienced leader on the floor during the dinner rush and overnight, not adding headcount.

## Oklahoma — what's happening

Night traffic is down 2.5–5.4% compared to last year across every recent window, but **labor cost is not the problem** — night labor% is already better than last year, and the district is running a sizable hours surplus against the staffing model (roughly 6,400 hours over the last 28 days, 17,500 over 90 days, 48,200 year-to-date). More scheduled hours doesn't buy speed either — stores with a bigger hours surplus don't run a faster drive-thru (the two barely move together at all).

The real issue is **drive-thru speed specifically getting worse** — up 9 to 18 seconds slower than a year ago across recent windows — while **kitchen speed has gotten faster** over the same stretch (down 8 to 11 seconds). That split points at something drive-thru-specific — equipment, headsets, car-holding discipline, order-taking staffing — not a general execution or staffing problem.

**Worst drive-thru speed gaps against target, dinner rush, last 28 days:** Sulphur (+95.6 sec), Pauls Valley-Ballard Rd (+94.4 sec), Chickasha-So 4th (+86.3 sec), Holdenville (+81.2 sec), Ada-Country Club (+73.2 sec).

**Two stores worth studying as reference points.** Duncan-Hwy 81 is the best-performing store over the last 90 days and year-to-date despite carrying a large hours surplus, but has drifted worse in just the last 28 days. Purcell is moving the opposite direction — it's closed a wide gap down to nearly on-target over the same stretch (from +34.8 sec year-to-date, to +22.2 sec over 90 days, to +9.5 sec in the last 28 days). Comparing what each is doing differently on the floor is the fastest way to find a transferable fix.

**Who's running the floor at night:** essentially every night shift in Oklahoma is covered by Swing Managers and crew — no exceptions. GMs show up at 0% of night shifts in the time-clock data, at any store, any hour, any day over the last 90 days — almost certainly because GMs are salaried and don't punch a time clock at all, not evidence they're never on-site. Department-Manager-level coverage — one step above the Swing Manager role, below GM — shows real activity at just 8 of the district's 27 stores, mostly at night; three of those are in Oklahoma — Lindsay-Wal-Mart, Atoka-Mississippi, and Holdenville — which gives a natural, already-in-progress comparison to start from rather than a blind pilot. Holdenville also just brought in a new GM this week, on top of that existing coverage — two real, already-in-motion comparisons at the same store worth tracking.

**Two** rough nights at Tishomingo (Oct 16–17) line up with a leadership change there — the GM who'd run it since 2025 moved up to a supervisor role in early October, and the store's new GM wasn't officially in place until Oct 22. Read as transition noise, not an open pattern — worth a light re-check once she's had a few normal weeks, nothing more.

## Florida — what's happening

Florida's night-shift problem is broader than Oklahoma's — it shows up on cost, speed, and productivity at nearly every store, not one metric at one store. **Night labor% runs 2.8 to 9.2 points over target at every single Florida store.** Night productivity (transactions per labor hour) misses target everywhere. Drive-thru speed at night is worse than target at 6 of the 7 stores.

**Freeport is the one to fix first.** It carries the worst night productivity gap and the worst night labor-cost overage in the state (nearly 5 points over target). On drive-thru speed specifically it's bad and getting worse — now +78 seconds over target in the last 28 days — but not literally the single worst on that one measure, since Mossy Head and Defuniak Springs have pulled even worse there (next point). **Mossy Head and Defuniak Springs are now tied for Florida's worst drive-thru speed problem at night, and it's getting worse, not better.** This was checked against a storm that hit the Florida panhandle Oct 9–12 and disrupted a couple of nearby stores' reopening days, and it isn't that — both stores' speed gaps have widened steadily across the last month, quarter, and year, well beyond that storm window (Defuniak: +60 sec year-to-date → +86 sec over 90 days → +113 sec in the last 28 days; Mossy Head similar). Mossy Head's version of this is a good problem to have — night sales and traffic are up double digits and still climbing, so this reads as demand outrunning the store's current night capacity, not a demand problem.

**Bonifay needs a direct look at a different issue first:** night sales and traffic there dropped sharply in just the last four weeks (down 13–19% versus last year) after running flat-to-even for the year — something changed recently and specifically at night, worth understanding before anything else. Separately, the manager who shows up in Bonifay's time-clock data at a department-manager level is exactly that — a genuine, hourly Department Manager, not a miscoded GM; nothing to run down there. Bonifay's GM changed in mid-October: the new GM started Oct 17, succeeding a GM who had run the store since 2023 and left a couple of days later — a clean handover, and its own natural before/after test worth tracking the same way as a formal pilot.

**Cottondale and Ponce de Leon are already beating their own night speed target**, consistently, across every recent window — the two bright spots in the state. Cottondale has the longer track record and is the stronger model to study; Ponce de Leon's result is newer and worth watching rather than banking on yet. Understanding what either is doing differently — crew assignment, drive-thru layout — is worth exploring to see if it transfers to the stores above.

**Who's running the floor at night:** six of Florida's seven stores run night shifts entirely on Shift Managers and crew, with no GM-or-above presence visible in the time-clock data — most likely because GMs are salaried and simply don't appear in that data, not necessarily that they're absent. A GM's actual *scheduled* shifts can also be checked directly through the scheduling system, for a window covering roughly the last three months — real but sparse (some stores show only a handful of weeks with a GM on the schedule at all) — so this is a real second source worth building out, not a dead end.

## What's true in both states

Every single one of the district's 27 stores runs weaker at night than during the day — no exceptions, in either state. But the shape of the problem is different: Oklahoma's is a drive-thru-speed problem with labor cost already under control; Florida's is a broader cost-and-productivity problem that shows up almost everywhere at once. Oklahoma's fix is likely process- or equipment-specific on the drive-thru; Florida's is more likely a scheduling/deployment issue, where staffing isn't coming down at close as fast as volume does.

**One finding that cuts across both states: every store's average ticket runs higher at night than during the day — all 27 of them, no exceptions.** District-wide, that premium averages +15.1% in Oklahoma and +10.7% in Florida (store range: from +18.1% at the high end to +5.5% at the low end).

&#91;embedded content: Average night-vs-day ticket premium by state, trailing 90 days, all 27 stores\]

This matters for how night productivity gets judged. The standard productivity target assumes the same average ticket all day, so a store earning more per ticket at night needs fewer transactions per labor hour to hit the same dollars-per-labor-hour the target was built around. Accounting for that narrows — but doesn't erase — the productivity gaps reported elsewhere in this review: Freeport's gap narrows from −30% to −19%, Tishomingo's from −23% to −9%, Holdenville's from −22% to −10%, and a couple of stores (including Defuniak Springs) flip from "missing" to "at or above" target once it's accounted for. Freeport still comes out the clear worst case either way — this adjustment changes the severity, not the overall story.

**Closing shifts are run by Swing/Shift Managers and crew in both states, with GMs effectively invisible to the clock-punch data at night in either market** — most likely because GM pay is salaried, not evidence of absence either way. The one lever with real, usable history behind it: 8 of the district's 27 stores already show real Department-Manager-level coverage on the books in the last 90 days (3 in Oklahoma, 5 in Florida), mostly at night — a faster, cheaper first comparison than starting a brand-new pilot everywhere.

**On testing a GM or district manager working more closing or mid shifts specifically:** the data available today can't prove or rule this out district-wide — committing to it everywhere on the strength of this review alone isn't warranted yet. The right next step is a bounded test: start at the 8 stores that already have real coverage and measure the before/after there first; where a genuinely new trial is warranted (chiefly Freeport in Florida, and the worst-gap Oklahoma stores), bound it to 2–3 closing shifts a week with that manager's daytime coverage explicitly backfilled — not a wholesale schedule change — and track both the night numbers and whatever happens during the day, so a night fix doesn't quietly become a day-shift problem.

## What we recommend doing next

This review tested every idea directly against 90 days of real store data — 2,369 individual store-nights — rather than assuming any of them true because they sound plausible. Two ideas were ruled out directly: scheduling more hours doesn't reliably buy speed or productivity anywhere in the district, and which manager happens to be more senior on a given night doesn't show a strong effect either, mostly because there's so little real seniority on the floor at night to begin with (the median Swing Manager on duty at night has been in the role well under a year, in both states). The idea that remains untested, and worth testing directly, is active senior-manager presence and coaching — not just who's scheduled, but who's actually working with the team.

**1. Start with the before/after comparisons already running, before launching anything new.** Eight stores across the district already have real Department-Manager-level management coverage in their recent schedules — three in Oklahoma (Lindsay-Wal-Mart, Atoka-Mississippi, Holdenville) and five in Florida (Mossy Head, Bonifay, Defuniak Springs, Ponce de Leon, Cottondale). Measure each one's own before/after, using this same review's method, before adding a single new trial anywhere. Holdenville and Bonifay also both just changed GMs, which is its own natural before/after test worth tracking the same way.

**2. Where a genuinely new trial is warranted, run it bounded and in both directions.** The clearest candidates with no existing coverage to lean on: Freeport in Florida, and the worst dinner-rush speed-gap stores in Oklahoma (Sulphur, Pauls Valley-Ballard Rd, Chickasha-So 4th, Holdenville, Ada-Country Club). Bound any new trial to 2–3 closing or mid shifts a week for the GM or district manager, with that manager's own daytime coverage explicitly backfilled — not a schedule takeover — and track both the night numbers and the daytime side of the trade, so this doesn't trade a night-shift fix for a day-shift problem. Watch every metric together — speed, productivity, labor% — not just one; a trial that only moves one number is a weaker result than one that moves all of them.

**3. In Oklahoma, go after drive-thru specifically — not labor hours.** The district already runs a large hours surplus with no payoff in speed, so adding scheduled hours isn't the fix. Investigate the drive-thru-versus-kitchen split store by store, starting with Sulphur, Pauls Valley-Ballard Rd, Chickasha-So 4th, Holdenville, and Ada-Country Club — likely causes are lane-specific: equipment, headsets, car-holding discipline, or drive-thru-specific crew staffing and skill. Use Duncan-Hwy 81 and Purcell as a paired comparison — one slipping, one improving fast — to find what's transferable between them.

**4. In Florida, fix Freeport first, then watch Mossy Head and Defuniak Springs closely.** Freeport carries the worst cost and productivity gap in the state. Mossy Head and Defuniak Springs have the worst, and still-worsening, drive-thru speed gap — confirmed as a real, standing problem rather than storm-related. Mossy Head specifically needs more night capacity to match demand that's already there, not a fix for a demand problem. Study Cottondale and Ponce de Leon, which already beat their own night speed target consistently, for anything that transfers.

**5. Look directly at what changed recently at Bonifay.** Night sales and traffic there dropped sharply in just the last month after running flat-to-even all year — worth understanding on its own, separate from the broader night-shift question. Also now explained, not a loose end: the manager showing up in hourly time-clock data is the store's genuine Department Manager, not a miscoded GM.

**6.** Tishomingo's two rough nights (Oct 16–17) — no action needed. They land inside that store's GM transition window; revisit only if it continues once the new GM is fully settled in.

**7. Put a real coaching and follow-up program under the Swing/Shift Manager role.** This review confirmed there currently is none — the only structured review cycle in place today is twice a year and covers salaried management only, not the role actually running nearly 100% of night shifts in either state. That's exactly the gap items 1 and 2 above are designed to test a fix for.

**8. Build a dedicated night-specific performance target.** Every comparison in this review is measured against a target built for the whole day blended together — the best yardstick available today, but an imprecise one. A target built the same way, just for the night window specifically, would sharpen every comparison above.

## How this was measured

Every comparison in this review measures the night window — roughly 4pm to close — against that same store's own daytime performance, and against existing company-wide productivity and labor-cost targets, using the most recent 28 days, 90 days, and year-to-date as of late October 2026. Dollar and rate figures are totaled across the full window and then divided — total dollars over total transactions, total hours over total sales — rather than averaged day by day, which is the correct way to measure a rate over a period and the basis for every percentage and dollar figure in this review.

Who's on the floor at night comes from payroll time-clock records, which reliably shows hourly staff but can't see salaried managers at all — a real limit of the data, flagged everywhere it matters rather than treated as proof that a manager was absent. The Florida dollar figure is a trailing-90-day measurement, extrapolated to a year for scale only — it is not a measured annual number and shouldn't be planned against as one.
